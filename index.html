Security architecture

Defence in depth. Nothing is stored in plain text. Ever.

Per-file keys & Secure Enclave

A unique AES-256 key is generated for every file. The master key is derived from your passphrase and stored in the iOS Keychain, protected by the device's Secure Enclave.

No tracking, no accounts

No analytics, no telemetry, no third-party SDKs, no account system, and no server I run. The only network connection the app ever makes is the optional iCloud sync you enable yourself — and even then, only ciphertext leaves your device. Nothing else leaves unless you explicitly export it.

Chunked streaming decryption

Videos are decrypted chunk-by-chunk on demand during playback. No temporary plaintext file ever hits the filesystem. Plaintext chunks are zeroed from memory after use.

App lifecycle protection

Privacy screen blacks out in the app switcher. Auto-lock on background. Face-down detection. Kill switch wipes keys from memory on any screen.

Brute-force lockout

Exponential back-off after three failed attempts — 5 seconds, then doubling, capped at 5 minutes. Lockout state survives app restarts.

Backup exclusion

Your vault is excluded from automatic iCloud and iTunes/Finder device backups — it's never swept up in a routine device backup. Moving a vault to another device is always deliberate: encrypted Backup-and-Restore, or the optional ciphertext-only iCloud sync.

Common Curiosities

Is it possible to hack a random 10-word passphrase?

For a truly random 10-word passphrase — the kind the built-in generator rolls for you — no, it isn't realistically possible. Not merely very hard: genuinely infeasible. The math: each word is picked at random from a 7,776-word list, so 10 words yields 7,77610 possible passphrases — a 39-digit number, about 8 × 1038 (roughly 129 bits). Even if an attacker could try a trillion guesses every second, working through that space would take about 25.6 quintillion years — nearly two billion times the current age of the universe. And that's the generous case: Armored Vault runs every guess through PBKDF2's 600,000 iterations, making each attempt hundreds of thousands of times slower still, and the app locks out after repeated failed attempts. There's no shortcut, either — AES-256 can't be reversed without the key, and no one (including me) holds a copy. The one catch: this only holds if the passphrase is genuinely random. A phrase you make up yourself — a quote, names, dates — has a tiny fraction of that strength and can fall fast, which is exactly why the app generates the words for you.

How do I create a strong passphrase?

Use the built-in Generate Passphrase tool in Settings. It rolls true random dice against the EFF Large Wordlist to build a Diceware passphrase of 7, 8, 10, or 12 words. A 10-word passphrase is ~129 bits — roughly 25.6 quintillion years to crack at a trillion guesses per second. You can also enter your own passphrase; a live strength meter rates it on the spot and the Save gate requires at least Strong (~80 bits).

What is AES-256-GCM?

It's the specific encryption every file in your vault is protected with — and it's the standard governments and banks rely on. AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) is the cipher the U.S. government adopted and Apple builds into its own hardware; 256 is the key length — 2256 possible keys, far more than any computer that exists or is on the horizon could ever brute-force; and GCM (Galois/Counter Mode) is an authenticated mode, so it doesn't only make your data unreadable, it also detects tampering: if even a single bit is altered, decryption fails instead of handing back corrupted data. In short, it delivers confidentiality and integrity in a single operation. Files are encrypted with it the instant they enter the vault, and the key never leaves your device's Secure Enclave.

What is PBKDF2 600K?

PBKDF2 (Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2) is how your passphrase becomes the key that actually encrypts your vault — and the 600K is what makes it tough to attack. Instead of using your passphrase directly, Armored Vault runs it through a one-way function 600,000 times, blended with a random per-vault salt. That deliberate slowness is the entire point: it makes every guess an attacker would try expensive to compute, turning a brute-force attack from feasible into effectively impossible — even on specialized cracking hardware. The derived key is never stored anywhere; it's rebuilt from your passphrase each time you unlock and wiped from memory the moment you lock. Under the hood it's PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512 at 600,000 iterations — well above common recommendations, and the same defense password managers and banks rely on.

What happens if I forget my passphrase?

AES-256 encryption cannot be reversed without your passphrase — your files cannot be decrypted by me, by Apple, or by anyone else. The app includes a Vault Reset Zone: tap "Forgot passphrase?" on the lock screen, authenticate with Face ID / Touch ID / device passcode, type RESET, and the current vault mode is wiped so you can start fresh. This is a clean-slate reset, not a decryption back door — your old encrypted files are permanently erased. Launch codes, app settings, the Intruder Log, and the other vault mode are preserved.

Why can I import from iCloud?

Armored Vault uses iOS's native Files picker for imports, so it can pull from any source iOS exposes — On My iPad, iCloud Drive, Dropbox, Google Drive, or anything else you've connected. The import is strictly one-way: files are copied in, encrypted on arrival, and from that moment live only on this device. This is separate from the optional iCloud sync feature — importing needs no account and no setup, and even with sync turned on, the only thing that ever moves through iCloud is ciphertext.

Is iCloud Sync private?

Yes. iCloud Sync is optional and off until you enable it. When it's on, only ciphertext leaves your device — encrypted file blobs and an encrypted index — through your own private iCloud. Apple never sees your file names, contents, or key, and there's still no account and no server of mine in between. One device is the Source and the rest are read-only Mirrors, so your devices can't drift apart or lose a change to a merge. Full detail in the Learning Center: How iCloud Sync Works.

What encryption does the app use?

AES-256-GCM — the same authenticated-encryption standard used by governments and financial institutions. Files are encrypted the moment they enter the vault. The master key is stored in the iOS Keychain, protected by the device's Secure Enclave.

Can I back up my vault?

Yes — four ways. Full Armored Vault Backup writes an encrypted snapshot of the entire vault to an external drive or any Files / Finder destination — pair with Restore from Backup on a new device to migrate, encrypted end-to-end. Full Decrypted Export writes every file decrypted to a drive in original formats. Export to Password-Protected Zip creates an AES-256 ZIP that opens in 7-Zip / Keka / WinZip; an optional Hide file names option seals everything inside a single ArmoredVault.zip so the outer archive's index reveals nothing. Export to ZIP is a plain unencrypted archive for trusted destinations.

What external drives are supported?

Any USB-C external SSD. For best results, format the drive as APFS on a Mac before use. APFS supports the full Unicode filename set, recovers cleanly from interrupted writes, and outperforms FAT-family filesystems on large videos.

Does the app work without internet?

Yes — completely. With sync turned off, Armored Vault has no online dependencies and never touches the network. Optional iCloud sync is the one feature that uses a connection, and it stays off until you enable it — leave it off and the app runs entirely offline, exactly as it always has.
